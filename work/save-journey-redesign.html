Redesigned the Save Journey for a UK savings & investing app, reversing a 7-month Autosave decline

Collapsing a two-step, wallet-mediated deposit flow into one action.

Summary

02

The Problem

Weekly Autosave-setup volume had been declining for roughly seven months before this project started. That aggregate trend was the signal that something in the funding path was blocking users — even though, without full event tracking on every screen, we couldn't yet say which step.

Every deposit and withdrawal in the app passed through an intermediate holding account — an internal “wallet” — before reaching a user's actual savings pot or investment account. To the user, this wasn't invisible plumbing; it was a real account they had to think about, fund, and wait on before their money reached the account they actually wanted.

Before: bank → wallet (step 1, wait for funds to land) → pot (step 2, manual action to move funds again).

The wallet existed for the business's operational convenience — it decoupled deposit timing from pot creation — not because the user needed a concept to reason about between “my bank” and “my savings.” Every extra step and every wait is a point where a user with a five-minute window abandons and never returns.

03

Diagnosis

Diagnosis without full instrumentation

Not every screen in the original flow had event tracking. I couldn't fully attribute the seven-month decline to a single step, so I worked from a stated hypothesis instead of a complete funnel:

Upgrade screen forces a mid-flow trade-offA plan-comparison screen asks users to weigh subscription cost against interest-rate uplift (e.g. Free at 3.46% AER vs Pro at 4.50% AER for £6.99/month) in the middle of an unrelated task — a natural drop-off point.
Wallet-first requirement adds real-world delayUsers had to deposit into the wallet, wait for funds to clear, then create a pot and set up Autosave — a day or two, long enough for intent to decay before returning.

I treated this as a testable assumption, not a certainty: I couldn't prove step-by-step attribution from the data available, so the design response was chosen to address both hypotheses at once — removing the wait, and, separately, not blocking pot creation behind a plan comparison — rather than betting the whole redesign on one diagnosis being right.

04

Alternatives Considered

Auto-sweep from the walletKept an unnecessary internal account and system state alive for no user-facing benefit — removing the wallet outright cut more steps than smoothing over the ones that remained.
One payment rail for every depositRouting by bank support alone broke for Autoinvest, which specifically requires Direct Debit — the right rail depends on the transaction, not just the bank.
05

The Solution

One flow, two rails

After: bank → pot, directly, in one action. The wallet still exists as internal plumbing behind the scenes, but the user never sees it, funds it, or waits on it as a separate step.

Open Banking made a faster default possible, but it doesn't work for every bank, and Autoinvest specifically requires Direct Debit — so I designed the deposit flow around a smart default rather than a single blanket rule.

Design decision

For onboarding, the payment rail is fully resolved by the system — nothing for the user to choose. For a one-off deposit, the system picks a smart default (Open Banking, or Direct Debit if the balance falls short) but leaves Direct Debit available as an explicit choice for anyone who wants it. Most users' job stays constant — tap one button — while the option to choose stays there for the ones who go looking for it.

I worked through this branching logic with AI — including edge cases like an in-flight Direct Debit payment landing after the balance check already fired — before handing the fully specified rules to engineering to build.

Deferring KYC Until Intent Is Confirmed

Alongside collapsing the deposit step, we also moved KYC (identity verification) later in the flow. Rather than front-loading it before a user could even choose a pot, KYC now runs only after the user has confirmed the pot's deposit amount and Autosave setup — the last step of the Save journey, not the first — and only triggers for users who haven't already passed KYC.

Two things follow from this. First, it directly addresses the earlier diagnosis: regulatory data collection was one of the heaviest, least reversible steps in the old flow, and pushing it past the point of stated intent means we only run — and pay for — KYC checks on users who have already committed to funding an account. Second, it lets the part of the flow users actually came for — setting the deposit amount and Autosave rules — complete faster, since it's no longer gated behind identity verification.

Timeline

By mid-June, this direction was already drafted and under review — the core shape of the redesign predates what happened next.

  • 21 May — first Figma draft (pot onboarding flow)
  • 18 June — updated draft (Autosave setup included), reviewed by Yukiko
06

The Forcing Function

This had already been scoped as a planned migration: phase the wallet out gradually, migrate existing balances, retire it on our own schedule. On 30 June, our banking infrastructure partner quietly blocked the creation of new wallet accounts, with no warning. Overnight, “planned migration” became “new users can't onboard the old way, starting now.” The redesign had to ship for new users immediately, while the fade-out for existing balance-holders continued in parallel on the original timeline.

It would have been easy to treat this purely as an outage to patch around. Instead, the forced timeline became a reason to fix things we already knew were wrong: collapsing two actions into one, giving every user a working funding path regardless of their bank, and replacing an accidental “front door” with deliberate entry points. The constraint didn't lower the bar on the solution — it removed the option of deferring it.

The plan accelerated:

  • 6–8 July — further revision covering the initial deposit and Autosave setup flow together, reviewed by Yukiko
  • 12–13 August — after engineering built the flow, a QA and copy pass on the shipped build, catching UX copy inconsistencies such as “Autosave” capitalization and “Set up” vs. “Setup”
07

Rollout

A fade-out, not a cutover

We couldn't delete the wallet for everyone on day one; some users still had real money sitting in it. The rollout split into two tracks running in parallel:

New users & zero-balance usersNever saw the wallet at all — went straight into the new one-step flow.
Existing users with a wallet balanceKept seeing the wallet, paired with in-context messaging and a feed prompt nudging them to move that money into a pot. As each balance emptied out, the wallet quietly disappeared for them too.
Design decision

The wallet had quietly doubled as the app's general-purpose “add money” entry point. Removing it removed that entry point along with the friction it caused, so alongside the redesign I added explicit Add money buttons directly on the Save and Invest tab home screens, letting a user fund a pot or investment account in one tap without first drilling into a specific pot.

08

Results & Economics

In the weeks following the redesign, the decline reversed: daily Autosave volume held at 5–6× its prior baseline for the following month — including a run of days that matched the app's previous busiest week of the year (early January), a signal the team read as evidence the new flow could sustain, not just spike, that level of activity.

The change also had a favourable financial shape: dropping the paid wallet-vendor relationship saves roughly £10,000–20,000/month, against a one-time cost in the tens of thousands for the banking partner to build the direct-funding API that enables Stage 2. The design case for the redesign stood on its own — the economics made the timing easier to justify internally, not the other way around.

09

Reflection

What's next

Stage 1 (shipped) was the design fix available on our timeline — it solved the user-facing problem immediately. Stage 2 (next) is the infrastructure fix: once our banking partner finishes building its own funding API, the wallet's remaining plumbing retires entirely, and we can fund the pot with no internal staging step at all.

Why this belongs in the portfolio

This project sits at the intersection of regulated financial infrastructure and interaction design: the constraint wasn't aesthetic, it was a payment-rail and KYC/regulatory reality that had to be resolved by the system rather than exposed to the user. The forcing function — a partner cutting off wallet creation with no warning — meant the design had to hold up under a compressed, non-negotiable timeline, and be phased safely against real user balances rather than shipped as a single cutover.
